The Geography of Happiness

Where we live, how we rate our lives—and what geography alone cannot explain.

This dataset has no measures of emotion or personal priorities, so this story covers life evaluation and its model components only.

Do people in different places rate their lives differently?

Life evaluation asks people to place their own life on a ladder from 0 (worst possible) to 10 (best possible). Each country’s score averages those answers; it describes how people judge their lives, not how they feel day to day. Start with the latest period, then drag the timeline back to 2019.

      Does geography explain the map?

      Temperature and latitude describe where countries are, but neither isolates geography from the social conditions that share its pattern. To describe a straight-line association, we use r: a correlation coefficient from −1 to +1; 0 means no linear relationship. First compare countries by long-term temperature, then read their position north or south of the equator.

      The global temperature pattern weakens after accounting for region and income. Among 144 countries with temperature data, the unweighted Pearson correlation between country temperature and life evaluation is −0.465. Partial Pearson correlations, calculated from residuals after regressing both variables on continent categories, are −0.098; adding the GDP model contribution gives 0.001. GDP contribution is a model proxy, not observed income, and these comparisons do not identify a climate effect. Costa Rica and Ethiopia have nearly the same mean temperature but different scores. Does distance from the equator tell us more?

      Latitude shows where the sample is concentrated. Across 147 countries, absolute latitude and life evaluation have Pearson r = 0.510; the partial correlation after accounting for continent is 0.163. The northern temperate zone contains 73 countries, including 39 in Europe. Only 28 countries lie south of the equator, 17 in Africa; broad latitude bands therefore mix geography with a very uneven regional sample. If place alone cannot settle the gap, what do the score’s components show?

      If geography does not explain it, what does?

      One component cannot explain the whole score. Kosovo and Yemen have nearly identical social-support contributions (1.293 and 1.292), but rank 16th and 142nd, with a 3.378-point score gap. Dystopia plus residual—the model’s reference level and unexplained remainder—accounts for 1.486 points of that gap (44%); GDP contribution accounts for 0.843 (25%), freedom 0.496 (15%) and health 0.476 (14%). These parts locate the difference without telling us why it exists.

      The score gap has several parts, and its largest part is unresolved. For Kosovo and Yemen, Dystopia plus residual is the largest modelled difference. It combines a reference level with what the model does not explain, so the bars locate the gap without identifying a social cause. The other case buttons ask whether similar climate or similar total scores can hide a different mix. What, then, can geography explain across the whole story?

      What did geography explain?

      Geography explains less than the map suggests. In the latest period, 2023–2025, life evaluation spans 6.318 points across 147 countries; regional gaps of about two points barely moved from 2019 to 2025. Colder countries score higher overall (r = −0.465), but r falls to −0.098 after accounting for continent and to 0.001 after also accounting for the GDP model contribution. Distance from the equator follows a similar pattern, from 0.510 to 0.163 when continent is accounted for. Kosovo and Yemen have nearly identical social-support contributions but scores 3.378 points apart; the model’s residual makes up 1.486 points of that gap. Costa Rica and Ethiopia show that similar climates can accompany different scores, while Singapore and El Salvador show that similar scores can conceal different component mixes. These are country averages, not individual lives; components are modelled contributions, not measured feelings. The dataset cannot reveal what people personally seek or feel. Place shapes starting conditions; it does not set the score.
